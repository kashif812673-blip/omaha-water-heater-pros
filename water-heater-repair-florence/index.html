Where Omaha's Water Story Began

Most of Omaha gets its water because of Florence. The Florence Water Works, built in 1879 and 1880 on what is now J.J. Pershing Drive, supplied water to both Florence and Omaha City when it opened. It was privately owned until the Metropolitan Utilities District was created in 1913, and M.U.D. acquired the Water Works in 1916. The Missouri River intakes built here in the 1910s still feed M.U.D. today, which means the water in a Florence home traces back more than a century to the works down the street.

That transfer is also, strangely, why Florence became part of Omaha at all. Losing the Water Works cost the town roughly 162,000 dollars a year in tax revenue. Florence went bankrupt, could not pay even 3,000 dollars in school bonds, and that bankruptcy led directly to its annexation by Omaha on June 1, 1917.

Here is why any of that matters to your water heater. That same M.U.D. water, drawn from the river and treated at works that started in Florence, is what your tank heats every single day, and it is hard, around 9 to 10 grains per gallon. Hard water is the single biggest enemy of a water heater, because the dissolved minerals drop out as the water heats and settle as sediment in the tank. Why Florence's Oldest Homes Are the Hardest on Water Heaters

Florence was founded in 1854, on the site of the 1846 Mormon Winter Quarters, and incorporated as a city in 1857, before Nebraska was even a state. The oldest house in Omaha, built in 1859, still stands at 8621 North 31st Street. Pre-1900 buildings are common here, and that age is exactly what makes Florence the hardest neighborhood in the metro on a water heater.

The supply lines are the first problem. Homes built before the 1960s often still carry galvanized steel supply lines, the silver-gray pipes that corrode from the inside out, restrict flow, and eventually leak. Corroded lines starve a water heater and speed up tank corrosion from within, so a new tank on old galvanized pipe is only a half-fix. In Florence, those old lines are the norm, not the exception, which is why your pro checks the plumbing feeding the tank on every call here.

Then there is the sediment. Omaha plumbers find the worst scale buildup in pre-1980 homes, where hard water deposits form far faster and water heater connections can narrow by as much as 70 percent of their capacity. Florence's age plus the city's hard water adds up to the heaviest sediment load of any neighborhood in Omaha, which is why the popping and rumbling of a sediment-packed tank is the classic Florence service call. Here, an annual flush is survival, not just maintenance. And because these are old homes with thin insulation, Omaha's freeze-thaw winters put extra stress on exposed piping, so no-hot-water calls climb in the cold and your pro checks exposed pipes and venting on winter visits.
