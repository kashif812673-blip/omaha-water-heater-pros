Why Millard Homes Are Hitting the Replacement Window

Millard was its own town until Omaha annexed it in 1971, and most of what you see today went up during the suburban boom that followed. The median home here was built in 1991, making Millard one of the newest neighborhoods in the city. That sounds like good news for water heaters, and for a while it was, but it also means a huge number of homes are reaching the same milestone at once: the original tank is now past, or close to, the 10 to 15 year life of a typical unit.

The plumbing behind the tank is in better shape than in Omaha's older neighborhoods. Homes from the 1970s through the 1990s were built with copper supply lines rather than the galvanized steel of a pre-war house. Copper lasts, but at 30 to 50 years old it can develop pinhole leaks, and Omaha's hard water, about 9 to 10 grains per gallon, still bakes sediment onto burners and elements. A Millard tank that was never flushed is exactly the kind that pops and rumbles. We check the lines and the water pressure on every visit, not just the tank.

The Polybutylene Check: What We Look for in 1978-1995 Millard Homes

If your Millard home was built between 1978 and 1995, there is one thing we check that most companies never mention. Many homes from that era were plumbed with polybutylene, a gray plastic supply pipe usually stamped PB2110. It was used in roughly one in four US homes built in that window, and it is now known to grow brittle from the inside over the years and fail suddenly. Millard's boom decades put a lot of it in the ground here.

On a water heater call in a home of that age, we look at the supply lines right at the tank for that gray pipe and the PB2110 stamp. Here is why it matters: putting a brand-new tank onto aging polybutylene lines leaves the real risk in place. We are not here to alarm you. We simply tell you what you have and what your options are, so you can plan for it on your terms rather than after a line lets go. No one else in Omaha is looking for this.
