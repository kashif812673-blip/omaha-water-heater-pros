Common Electric Water Heater Problems in Omaha

An electric unit usually tells you something is wrong through one of a few symptoms. Here is what each one points to.

No hot water at all. On an electric unit this usually means a tripped breaker, a burned-out heating element, or a failed thermostat. The heating element is the metal component that heats the water directly; when it burns out, the water stays cold. We find which of the three it is before replacing anything.

Not enough hot water, or it runs out fast. Often a failed lower element, so only the top of the tank heats, or a broken dip tube, the tube that sends incoming cold water to the bottom of the tank to be heated. Sediment from Omaha's hard water baking onto the lower element is another common cause.

Water is hot for a few minutes, then goes cold. This is the classic upper-element-working, lower-element-failed pattern. The top of the tank heats, but once that is used up the failed lower element cannot keep up, so you get a short burst of hot water and then cold.

The breaker keeps tripping. A heating element that has shorted to ground, or faulty wiring, will trip the breaker when the unit tries to heat. This is covered in its own section below, because it is a safety issue.

Temperature swings, too hot or too cold. Usually a failing thermostat, the control that sets and holds the water temperature. We test it under load rather than just reading the dial.

Electric Water Heater Components Our Local Pros Repair in Omaha

When an electric unit fails, it is almost always one of these parts. Here is what each one does, in plain terms.

Upper and lower heating elements. The two metal elements that heat the water directly, one near the top of the tank and one near the bottom. The lower one does most of the work and fails most often, especially with sediment buildup. A burned-out element is the single most common electric repair.

Upper and lower thermostats. The controls that set and hold the water temperature and tell each element when to fire. A failed thermostat gives you water that is too hot, too cold, or swinging between.

High-limit switch and reset button. The high-limit switch is a safety cutoff that shuts the unit down if the water gets dangerously hot. The reset button (sometimes called the ECO) is the red button that pops when that cutoff trips. A button that keeps popping means an underlying fault, not a one-time glitch.

Dip tube. The tube that carries incoming cold water down to the bottom of the tank so it heats properly. A cracked or broken dip tube mixes cold water in at the top, so your hot water turns lukewarm fast.

Wiring and connections. Loose or corroded electrical connections can cause intermittent heating or trip the breaker. Your pro checks these as part of the diagnosis.

Pros carry the common parts, so most of these are a one-visit repair.

Breaker Tripping or Reset Button Popped?

A tripping breaker and a popped reset button are the two things that scare people most, and for good reason. Here is what each one means and the safe way to handle it.

If the breaker trips once, you can reset it a single time. If it trips again right away when the heater tries to heat, stop. A breaker that keeps tripping is doing its job, telling you there is an electrical fault, usually a heating element that has shorted to ground or a wiring problem. Do not keep flipping it back on, because you are overriding a safety device. Call us instead.

If the red reset button on the unit keeps popping, that is the high-limit switch cutting power because the water got too hot. Pressing it back in once is fine, but if it pops again, there is a failed thermostat or high-limit switch behind it, and resetting it repeatedly is unsafe. One reset is troubleshooting; a second pop is a service call. Call [PHONE] and we will test it under load and find the real cause.

Repair or Replace Your Electric Water Heater?

Most electric faults are repairable, and a repair is usually the right call on a sound tank. Electric tank water heaters typically last 8 to 12 years. A burned-out element, a failed thermostat, or a popped reset switch on an otherwise good tank is a repair, not a reason to replace. Replacement becomes the better option when the tank itself is leaking or rusting from the inside, or when a unit near the end of that age range is stacking up repairs.
