What a Tankless Installation Actually Involves in Omaha

This is the part most companies skip, and it is the part that matters. Converting a tank to a tankless unit is not a like-for-like swap, because a tankless unit works completely differently, and a few real engineering steps come with it. Treating the changeover as a simple appliance swap is how a unit ends up starved of gas, short-cycling, and failing early.

Gas line upsizing. A tankless unit fires much harder than a tank, often up to 199,000 BTU, which is far more gas than a tank draws. Many conversions need the gas line upsized, commonly from half-inch to three-quarter or one inch, so the unit can reach full output instead of starving.

New venting. A tankless unit vents differently from a tank. A condensing unit (the high-efficiency type that pulls extra heat out of its own exhaust) uses sealed venting and produces an acidic condensate that has to drain through neutralizing media (a cartridge that neutralizes the acid before it drains) to meet code.

Freeze-proof venting and placement. In an Omaha winter, the venting and any unit near an exterior wall or unheated space has to be protected so it cannot freeze, because a frozen tankless unit can crack.

Scale prevention. Omaha's hard water scales a tankless heat exchanger faster than it affects a tank, so a conversion should include a plan to keep scale out from day one.

We handle all of this as part of the install, so you get a tankless system that works at full output and lasts, not one forced into a tank's old connections.

Sizing a Tankless Unit for Omaha Winters

A tankless unit is sized differently from a tank, and Omaha's climate is the reason sizing matters here. Instead of a gallon capacity, a tankless unit is rated by two numbers working together: flow rate, which is how many gallons per minute it can heat at once, and temperature rise, which is how far it can raise the incoming water's temperature.

Temperature rise is where Omaha winters come in. In summer the groundwater coming into your home might be close to 60 degrees, but in the depth of an Omaha winter it can drop near 40 degrees. The colder that incoming water, the harder the unit has to work to reach a comfortable shower temperature, which lowers how much hot water it can deliver at once. A unit sized for a mild day will fall short in January. We size for the winter worst case and your peak demand, how many showers, sinks, and appliances might run hot at the same time, so the unit keeps up all year.

Gas Line and Venting Requirements

A gas tankless unit has real requirements a tank does not, and meeting them is what separates a unit that performs from one that struggles. The gas line often has to be upsized, commonly to three-quarter or one inch, so the unit gets enough fuel to fire at full output. The venting has to match the unit: a condensing unit uses sealed, concentric venting (a dual pipe that pulls fresh combustion air from outside while it exhausts), and it needs a condensate drain. Combustion air and clearances have to be right so the unit breathes and vents safely.

An electric tankless unit skips the gas and venting but has its own demand: it needs one or more dedicated circuits of high capacity, and a whole-house electric tankless unit often requires an electrical service upgrade to supply it. We confirm what your unit and your home need, and handle the gas, venting, or electrical as part of the install.

Protecting Your Tankless Unit from Omaha's Hard Water

Omaha's water, supplied by the Metropolitan Utilities District (M.U.D.), is hard, measured around 10 grains per gallon in much of the metro, and that hardness is tougher on a tankless unit than on a tank. A tankless heat exchanger runs water through narrow passages at high heat, which is exactly where minerals drop out and harden into scale. Left unaddressed, that scale chokes the exchanger, triggers error codes, shortens the unit's life, and can void the manufacturer warranty.

So we build protection in at install time, not as an afterthought. Depending on your home, that means pairing the unit with a water softener or a catalytic scale inhibitor (a device that keeps the minerals from bonding into scale), plus setting you up for the annual descaling a tankless unit needs in Omaha. Protecting the unit from day one is what lets it reach the 20-year-plus lifespan tankless units are known for.
